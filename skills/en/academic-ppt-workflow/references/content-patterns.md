# Content Pattern Library

In Steps 2–3, use the index to select and read the entries that fit the audience and teaching purpose. In Step 4, apply the chosen patterns' criteria to check whether the explanation is clear. A pattern may span one slide or several consecutive slides; it is not a fixed-coordinate template, and a presentation need not include every type. Combine patterns when their responsibilities overlap. If none fits, record a custom organizing logic instead of forcing a match.

Extend the library using the structure “applicability — narrative and required information — slides/titles/layout — notes — acceptance and exceptions.” Keep information that changes planning or review behavior rather than accumulating synonymous layouts. The master content record may simply reference a pattern. Production, authorization, and reconstruction rules remain in the main workflow and visual production reference.

## Content and speaker notes

Slides emphasize key facts. During construction, put detailed statistical scope, calculation methods, source locations, and expanded explanations in the corresponding **PPT speaker notes**; external Markdown does not replace actual notes. When delivering only an outline, save notes for later import and do not claim they have already been written into a PPT. Slides may use short source IDs, with full locations in the notes.

If removing an explanation would change the meaning of a number or conclusion, retain the accurate term or shortest necessary qualification on the slide. Other explanation may go in notes. For example, a ransom demand is not an incurred loss, and expected gains are not measured gains. Record facts, inferences, invented scenarios, and illustrative data separately. If a visual could be mistaken for an actual result, label its nature on the slide, such as “illustration”; do not require a warning box on every slide.

## Real-world incident cases

- **Applicability:** Use real events supported by reliable sources and relevant to the argument to explain practical impact. Multiple cases should add different consequences or conditions. Do not present a research demonstration as a real-world incident.
- **Narrative and required information:** Time/background → key events → verified impact → relevance to the topic. Emphasize economic loss, scale of exposure, service disruption, or actual benefit. When reliable numbers are unavailable, emphasize supported qualitative consequences rather than exaggerating figures for visual effect. Distinguish multifactor events from effects attributable to a single technology.
- **Slides and presentation:** Usually one event per slide; split complex chains across slides. Consistent titles such as “Event: key change/consequence” support comparison. Arrange the process and news/report image side by side or vertically, making the impact visually prominent without a fixed ratio.
- **Assets and notes:** Associate each case with its display intent, candidate or pending selection status, placeholder area/aspect ratio, and planned insertion stage. Keep a provisional placeholder when no image has been found, then calibrate it after verification. Summaries and generation prompts inherit this plan. Create placeholders and later insert images according to [Authentic images and placeholders](visual-production.md#authentic-images-and-placeholders). Put full sources, numeric definitions, and attribution explanations in notes; retain necessary qualifications on the slide.
- **Acceptance and exceptions:** The process, impact, and evidence must correspond. Do not present potential risk as an incurred loss. A news image does not automatically substantiate a number. Omitting an image because it is inapplicable or the user requests omission requires a specific basis; “no asset list” or “could not find an image” does not mean the task is complete.

## Methods and principles

- **Applicability:** Explain how a technique, research method, algorithm, operating mechanism, or complex business process works. For nonexperts, or when abstract objects or multistep changes are involved, use a “principle + concrete example” unit by default.
- **Narrative and required information:** The principle explains prerequisites, the object acted on/modified, the goal, key steps, output, and limitations. For iteration, explain candidates/updates, feedback, and stopping conditions. The example gives a concrete input or scenario and shows key intermediate changes, the result, and how these correspond to the principle's steps. “Give several examples,” “repeat until successful,” or a question mark does not constitute a worked example.
- **Slides and presentation:** For complex material, normally separate principle and example slides. Use a structure/process diagram for the principle and a concrete flow, timeline, input/output comparison, or single iteration for the example. Titles might be “Method 1: core mechanism” and “Method 1 example: how one input changes.” Parallel methods should share comparable title structures and explanatory order while using graphics suited to their own relationships.
- **Comparable conditions:** Keep the example background, goal, and input consistent across methods where possible; explain unavoidable differences. Every method needing a worked example must have one, rather than supplying an example for only one method. A combined exercise tests understanding and can serve as examples only if it actually shows each method's complete process.
- **Notes:** Include derivations, details, simplifying assumptions, and optional delivery suggestions. Keep the essential principle and example steps on the slides rather than hiding them all in notes. Identify real samples, invented scenarios, and illustrative data. Without actual testing, do not present illustrative responses or numbers as real outputs or measured effects.
- **Acceptance and exceptions:** The audience can use the example to explain why the steps produce the result. The example matches the mechanism; an illustration does not prove real effectiveness. Simple methods may fit their principle and example on one slide. Examples may be omitted for an expert overview or an explicit user exclusion, with an explanation. Do not force this pattern onto pure results/decision slides. Approximate duration or self-estimated slide counts do not justify removing necessary examples; handle hard constraints through the main workflow.

## Comparing alternatives

- **Applicability:** Overview or compare methods, products, or approaches to establish differences or prepare a choice. First clarify whether the slide provides background positioning, the comparison itself, or navigation for later item-by-item explanations.
- **Narrative and required information:** Shared problem → describe every option along the same dimensions → key differences → applicable conditions. Compare dimensions that actually explain differences, such as the core idea, object, conditions, or cost. Do not mix goals, means, and outcomes as mutually exclusive categories.
- **Slides and presentation:** Use parallel tables/columns or a chart with a common scale. Keep titles and dimension order consistent within the group. Complex options may be compared first and explained individually afterward; an overview does not replace promised detail.
- **Notes:** Expand comparison definitions, evidence sources, and condition differences. For a short talk, prioritize key rows while preserving the full material.
- **Acceptance and exceptions:** Give options comparable depth, explaining empty entries. Do not force rankings across unequal conditions or invent strengths/weaknesses for visual symmetry.

## Processes and system relationships

- **Applicability:** Explain work steps, changes, data/control flows, or component relationships. These are not necessarily methods needing worked examples.
- **Narrative and required information:** Starting point/boundary → stage or component responsibilities → transfer/state change → result. Include branches, conditions, feedback, and exceptions as the actual content requires.
- **Slides and presentation:** Use flowcharts, swimlanes, timelines, or architecture diagrams. The title states the process being shown. Distinguish temporal, causal, data, and control arrows rather than drawing every relationship as the same pipeline.
- **Notes:** Include interface assumptions, exceptional branches, and implementation details. Put conditions that affect understanding on the relevant edge or next to the node.
- **Acceptance and exceptions:** The audience can trace the path from entry to result, with clear cross-component responsibilities and no broken paths or reversed feedback. Do not invent a temporal sequence for a static architecture or add loops to a noniterative process.

## Results and arguments

- **Applicability:** Present research findings, project outcomes, or business evidence to support a claim.
- **Narrative and required information:** Question/claim → data or other real evidence → interpretation → applicability and limitations. Distinguish expected from measured benefits, and observations from causal inferences.
- **Slides and presentation:** Give the evidence graphic prominence and pair it with a concise conclusion. Complex results may be split into “results — interpretation.” Use conclusion-style titles when evidence supports them. Authentic charts follow the rules for data and original-image fidelity.
- **Notes:** Include sample scope, statistical definitions, calculation methods, and source locations. Keep conditions essential to the conclusion's meaning on the slide.
- **Acceptance and exceptions:** Charts genuinely support the claim without selectively omitting key conditions. Do not generate performance curves without measured data. A results-only report does not require extra teaching slides for principles and examples.

## Evaluation and acceptance

- **Applicability:** Judge effectiveness, risk, quality, or whether an approach meets requirements.
- **Narrative and required information:** Evaluation goal → metric definition → judgment/measurement method → comparison conditions. Distinguish “what is measured,” “how the judgment is obtained,” and “how fairness is ensured.” Add cost, error, or usability on normal tasks when necessary.
- **Slides and presentation:** For a small amount of information, divide the slide by responsibility; for complex material, separate metric and method slides. Use decision flows, formulas, and condition tables. Do not put metrics, manual/automated labeling, and controlled variables into one flat list.
- **Notes:** Include denominators/statistical units, budgets, sampling checks, and methodological limitations. Show key thresholds or success definitions on-slide as needed.
- **Acceptance and exceptions:** There is a clear path from input/observation to judgment, with consistent metric definitions. Do not substitute a proxy for actual effectiveness. Without data, explain the evaluation design rather than inventing comparative results.

## Responses and improvements

- **Applicability:** After identifying a problem, explain how a defense, fix, or improvement works.
- **Narrative and required information:** Problem/failure point → corresponding measure → intervention location and responsibility → limitations → validation. Distinguish reducing occurrence probability, limiting consequences, and recovering afterward.
- **Slides and presentation:** Use problem-to-measure mappings, layered system diagrams, or repair-and-validation loops. Titles identify the problem being addressed. Order measures to correspond to earlier failure points or system layers.
- **Notes:** Include implementation conditions, costs, evidence strength, and regression testing methods. Do not present recommendations as already experimentally validated.
- **Acceptance and exceptions:** Each measure maps to a problem and a checkable effect. Avoid generic advice lists or claims that a single measure works universally. A one-sentence limitation/tip about a method can remain on its principle slide; reconsider section placement for a complete response plan.

## Decision recommendations

- **Applicability:** Help the audience choose an approach, approve action, or understand tradeoffs.
- **Narrative and required information:** Goal and hard constraints → candidates and evidence → tradeoffs → recommendation and applicable conditions. Add actions or items needing validation when necessary.
- **Slides and presentation:** Use comparison matrices, decision trees, or recommendation cards. Keep the title focused on the decision rather than automatically turning it into technical instruction.
- **Notes:** Include assumptions, weights, budget definitions, evidence gaps, and alternative details.
- **Acceptance and exceptions:** The recommendation follows from evidence, meets hard constraints, and states unknowns honestly. Do not force an expansion into mechanisms and examples when the user only needs a decision brief.

## Summary and reflection

- **Applicability:** Close the main explanation, bring together the narrative of a research, business, product, or teaching presentation, and introduce transfer questions or next steps.
- **Narrative and required information:** Return to the goal/main narrative → key takeaways and boundaries → reflection/application questions or follow-up actions. Distinguish summarizing established conclusions from introducing new main content.
- **Slides and presentation:** A summary may use a few parallel statements or a narrative diagram. Reflection may use scenarios/questions; separate them when complex. Follow the main workflow for agenda placement and a standalone transition for a multislide concluding block. A short single-slide summary can signal closure through its own visual hierarchy.
- **Notes:** Link each point to earlier evidence and plan interaction answers, transitions, and timing tradeoffs. Do not hide essential new knowledge exclusively in summary notes.
- **Acceptance and exceptions:** The audience can tell that the main content has ended, and earlier material supports the conclusions. Reflection depends on content already explained. Closing thanks/Q&A and references have separate roles and do not replace a summary. Not every deck needs an interactive question.
